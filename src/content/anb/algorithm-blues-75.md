AI-generated meeting summaries are becoming pretty commonplace. It's easy to understand the appeal. Nobody has to take notes, decisions and action items are neatly organized, and there's a written account to refer to afterward. Even people who attended the meeting will eventually rely on that account rather than their own recollection of the conversation. I hadn't really considered whether reading a summary might actually change what someone remembers about the meeting. Some recent research suggests that's worth thinking about.

Researchers Mattea Sim, Yael Eiger, and Tadayoshi Kohno explored that question in a [recent study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.28820) involving 328 participants. They showed people short animated videos of traffic accidents, each involving a car approaching an intersection marked by either a stop sign or a yield sign. A day or two later, participants read a summary of what they'd watched. Some received an accurate description of the traffic sign, while others were given a summary that identified the wrong sign.

When asked to recall what they'd actually seen, 83.6% of those who received an accurate summary remembered the sign correctly. Among those who read the misleading version, accuracy dropped to 44.8%. The participants had all watched the original video, yet the description they encountered afterward made a substantial difference in what they remembered.

There's a long history of psychological research showing how information acquired after an event influences memory. What's interesting here is how ordinary the intervention was. Participants simply read a summary of something they'd already experienced. The researchers also tested whether knowing the summary came from AI made a difference. Half the participants were told it was AI-generated; the others were told it came from a human professional. In reality, all the summaries were produced by AI, and the researchers found no statistically significant difference between the groups.

That last finding is particularly relevant as AI-generated records become more common. People generally understand that AI makes mistakes, and having attended a meeting would seem to put someone in a good position to recognize an inaccurate account. The research suggests that firsthand experience provides less protection than we might expect.

The researchers also examined the accuracy of the summaries themselves. They asked two AI systems to describe the accident videos, generating 20 summaries in total. Every summary contained errors, and 19 of them omitted the collision between the car and the pedestrian. The systems described the circumstances surrounding the accident while leaving out the accident itself.

This was a limited test involving only two short videos, so the results don't establish how often comparable mistakes occur in other applications. The researchers also deliberately altered the traffic sign description in the separate memory experiment, using an otherwise carefully selected and lightly edited summary. Still, the two parts of the research are worth considering together. AI systems sometimes produce inaccurate accounts, and people who encounter misleading accounts may subsequently remember events differently.

## When the Record Becomes the Reference

For businesses, the implications extend beyond whether a meeting summary accurately captures the discussion.

Organizations have always depended on written records to maintain some continuity of knowledge. Decisions get documented, project histories accumulate, and people joining an effort rely on earlier accounts to understand how things developed. Much of that information has traditionally been assembled by people who participated in the work, often with considerable effort.

AI is making that process easier and much more extensive. Meetings, customer conversations, support cases, and operational incidents are increasingly summarized automatically. Those summaries become available to people who weren't involved in the original activities, and eventually serve as source material for other reports and analyses.

Consider something as ordinary as a project committee discussing a proposed change. The participants identify several unresolved concerns and agree to reconsider the proposal after further investigation. The AI summary records the discussion as a conditional approval. It's a relatively small difference in wording, but an important one.

Someone preparing a status report uses the summary and reports that approval has been granted. A later project review relies on that report. By the time the committee revisits the subject, the supposed approval has become part of the project's documented history.

The example is hypothetical, and the study didn't examine organizational decision-making. But the possibility interests me because of the way businesses already use accumulated records. An inaccurate account doesn't necessarily remain confined to its original document. It becomes part of the information people use to interpret subsequent events, including those who were present when the original discussion occurred.

There's another complication as organizations begin using AI to retrieve and reason over their historical information. A question about why a decision was made may be answered using meeting summaries, project updates, and management reports that all ultimately originated from the same inaccurate account. The apparent agreement among those records offers little assurance if they share the same mistake.

It's easy to imagine that happening without anyone making a particularly careless decision. Each person relies on an account that appears reasonable, and the original conversation becomes increasingly distant.

I wouldn't take the findings as a reason to abandon AI-generated summaries. They're useful, and the research doesn't establish that they're more likely than human-written summaries to distort memory. The experiment also leaves open how the effect would play out in longer, more complicated business discussions.

What it does raise is a question about how much authority these records should acquire.

For consequential decisions, I'd want a clearer distinction between the generated account and the underlying evidence. Confirming decisions while participants are still together, preserving unresolved objections, and maintaining access to original recordings or documents would provide some protection against errors becoming accepted history. The familiar practice of having someone review the notes remains worthwhile, although the research gives us reason to be cautious about relying entirely on that person's memory.

## An Organization's Memory

There's also a broader issue here that I think deserves attention. Businesses are beginning to assemble institutional knowledge at a scale that would have been impractical only a few years ago. Much of that knowledge will come from AI interpretations of everyday activities, and future systems will increasingly rely on those interpretations to answer questions, make recommendations, and carry out work.

An organization might eventually know far more about its own activities than anyone working there could reasonably remember. How accurately it understands those activities will depend, in part, on what was preserved when the records were created.

The prospect of AI helping organizations remember their own history is appealing. I'm less certain we've given enough thought to what happens when the history it remembers isn't quite what happened.
